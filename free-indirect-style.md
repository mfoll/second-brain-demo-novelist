# Free Indirect Style

Third-person narration that takes on a character's own words and turns of thought without quotation marks or a "she thought". The narrator's grammar, the character's mind. The reference definition I give students is the Wikipedia article on [[free-indirect-speech]], which also gives the French name, *discours indirect libre*, the name I learned it under as a translator.

## What it is, in my terms

Three versions of the same moment for Nell at the telegraph desk:

- Direct: "It's the gale warning again," she thought.
- Indirect: She thought that it was the gale warning again.
- Free indirect: The gale warning again. Third time since Sunday, and nobody down the harbour would pay it any mind.

The third keeps "she" and the past tense, but the rhythm, the "again", the "down the harbour" and the judgement are Nell's. The narrator has stepped into her and not said so.

## How I use it in The Salt Choir

- **[[nell-garrick|Nell]]**: heavily. She is nineteen, quick, local, and her mind is full of the town's phrases, which she half mocks. Free indirect style lets the town's idiom into the narration through her without dialect spelling.
- **[[tobias-arden|Tobias]]**: sparingly, and less as the book goes on. His thoughts are not always sayable even to himself. When he is at his worst I pull the narration back out ([[narrative-distance]]), and the distance is the symptom. [[femi-adewale]]'s note that modern trauma vocabulary had leaked into Tobias's interior came from exactly here: free indirect style imports the character's words, so the words had better be his and of 1925.
- **[[constance-leal|Constance]]**: never. She speaks in first person in letters ([[letter-chapters]]). That contrast is part of the design: two characters we are inside, one who tells us only what she chooses.

## Pitfalls I have hit

- Leaking the author's vocabulary into a character: in draft one Nell "noted the irony", which she would never have said. Rule: if a word in a free indirect passage is mine, not hers, it goes.
- Leaking period vocabulary too thickly: the opposite fault ([[period-diction]]).
- Losing the reader about who is thinking in a scene with two viewpoint characters. In the third draft each chapter stays with one mind.
- Ambiguous irony: the reader cannot tell if the narrator shares a character's judgement. Sometimes useful; mostly a mistake.

## Teaching it

It was the centre of the second session of the [[evening-class-spring-2026|spring 2026 class]]. The handout uses one of my sentences in the three forms above, then a paragraph from [[mrs-dalloway]] for students to mark where the narration slides into Clarissa. The exercise that works: rewrite a paragraph of your own in strict indirect speech, then take every "she thought" out and see what has to change. Most students discover they were already doing it.
