# Research Iceberg

The working assumption that most of what I research never reaches the page, and that this is the research doing its job, not a waste of it. The reader should feel the weight under the water without being shown it.

## Where it comes from

Notebook nine, 2023, while translating a 1950s novel whose author clearly knew everything about fishing boats and showed almost none of it. I wrote "she knows the boat; she does not tell me the boat". The image of the iceberg is the usual one, borrowed from talk about Hemingway; the application to research is mine.

## How I use it

- Before a research trip, decide what question the chapter needs answered. Everything else is iceberg.
- After drafting, count research details per page in the chapter. In the first draft's curing-yard chapters it was eight or nine; in the third draft, two or three.
- The lifeboat research is the test case: weeks of reading and an archive day ([[merrin-archive-visit-2026-05-12]]) produce, on the page, oars, a horse team and forty minutes from maroon to afloat.

## Limits

Some readers want the homework, and some genres reward it. Historical fiction readers vary. I would rather lose the ones who want a lecture. The practical test sits in [[period-texture-vs-period-trivia]].
