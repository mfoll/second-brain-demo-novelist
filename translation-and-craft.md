# Translation And Craft

Twenty-two years of translating from French and Portuguese, about thirty books, before Lantern Hours. This Topic collects what that trade taught me about writing and what it still does to my prose: the habit of hearing a sentence twice, the suspicion of my own first adjective, and the way a translator's patience can turn into a novelist's timidity.

## What Translation Gave The Novel

- Constance Leal's letters work partly because I spent years making other people's letters sound like one person on a page. The class exercise that started them is in [[class-session-2026-01-27]].
- Free indirect style, which I teach and use for Nell and Tobias ([[free-indirect-style]]), is the mode I translated most often from French, where it is far more at home than in English.
- The anachronism habit: checking that a word existed in its period is a translator's reflex. See the [[saltmarrow-anachronism-log]].

## What It Costs

My first draft of The Salt Choir was correct, careful and flat in places, which [[imogen-lacey]] put more kindly in her editorial letter. Part of that is the translator's instinct to serve a text rather than push it. My stance on taking translation work while drafting is in [[translation-only-between-books]], now under revision because of the Ashlar offer.
