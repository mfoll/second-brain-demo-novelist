# Reverse Outline

An outline written from a finished draft rather than before it. One line per scene or section: what happens, whose viewpoint, what changes by the end. It shows the draft's actual structure, as opposed to the one the writer remembers planning.

## How I Teach It

From [[reverse-outline-handout-2026-10-06]]:

1. Read the draft through without editing.
2. One line per scene, in the order the reader meets them. No more than fifteen words per line.
3. Mark each line with the viewpoint and with what changes. A scene where nothing changes gets a question mark.
4. Read the outline alone. Look for repeated scenes, scenes with question marks, and threads that stop.
5. Only then plan the revision.

Optional appendix, with his agreement, from [[samir-benhaddou]]: a spreadsheet with columns for each thread (for crime, clue planted and clue paid off).

## What It Finds

- Repetition, especially two scenes doing the same job from different viewpoints. That was the problem in part one of my own third draft (Tobias and Nell repeating each other).
- Order problems: effects before causes.
- Material that belongs to another book ([[olu-bankole]]'s question in [[class-session-2026-10-06]]).

## Limits

It flattens voice; an outline of Constance's letters reads like a list of errands. It is a structural tool and should not be the basis of line revision.
