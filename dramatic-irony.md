# Dramatic Irony

The reader knows something a character does not, so every line the character speaks carries a second meaning. It is the effect a prologue sets up for a whole book.

## Why it matters here

With the [[storm-prologue]] in place, every lifeboat scene in parts one and two is read in the knowledge that a boat will go out in a storm in November 1927. A crewman joking about his new boots is no longer a joke. That is what the prologue is for.

## The risk

Dramatic irony sustained over 200 pages can harden into dread, then into impatience. [[teodora-vlasic]] read part one of the third draft "waiting for the boat" ([[teodora-reads-part-one-2026-09-09]]); she meant it as praise, Callum quoted it as a warning. The difference between the two is whether the waiting is also full of other things: Nell's job, the choir, the herring.

## Working rules

- Never wink. Characters must not say things that only make sense with the reader's knowledge.
- The prologue names nobody, so the irony attaches to the whole crew, not one man.
- Give the reader at least one thread in each chapter that is not about the storm.
