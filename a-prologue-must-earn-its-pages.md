# A Prologue Must Earn Its Pages

## Stance

A prologue is justified only if it gives the reader a question that every following chapter makes sharper, and costs no more than a few pages. Background, atmosphere or a stylish set piece are not enough. The Salt Choir's four-page storm prologue earns its place; a longer one would not.

## Scope

Literary novels where the main event comes late. Not genre conventions where a prologue is expected and readers skip it, and not frame stories ([[frame-story]]), which are a different device.

## Why I think this

- Most prologues I edited or translated were notes for the author dressed as an opening.
- The braid of draft 2 was a 200-page prologue in effect, and spent the storm early ([[salt-choir-structure-history]]).
- The current prologue tested on [[teodora-vlasic]] made her read part one with a held breath, which is the effect I want ([[teodora-reads-part-one-2026-09-09]]).

## What would change my mind

- Teodora or another first reader reporting, after part two, that the waiting became impatience rather than tension.
- Imogen withdrawing her support once she reads the whole third draft.
- Evidence from [[callum-pryce]] that readers of comparable books felt cheated. He is looking for it.

## Implications

Keep the prologue at four pages or under, without names or outcome. If it goes, the first chapter must carry the sea instead.
